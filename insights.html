Agentic QE • April 2025 • 8 min read

Why Agentic QE Is the Next Frontier

Quality engineering has always been reactive. We write tests after code is written. We run those tests after changes are made. We fix what breaks. This model worked when software changed slowly. It is breaking under AI-powered development, where code generation accelerates faster than human testers can adapt.

Agentic QE flips this model. Instead of humans writing tests that machines run, machines reason about quality end-to-end — generating test cases from requirements, executing them autonomously, adapting when the application under test changes, and surfacing insights that humans act on. The human role shifts from execution to judgment.

What makes a QE system "agentic"?

An agentic system has three properties: autonomy (it can take actions without per-step human instruction), goal-directedness (it pursues a defined objective across multiple steps), and adaptability (it updates its plan based on what it observes). Most test automation tools have none of these. They run a predefined script and report pass or fail.

💡 Key insight: The value of agentic QE is not speed — it's coverage. Agents explore application state spaces that humans never would, finding edge cases that matter precisely because they don't share human assumptions about what "normal" looks like.

The architecture

Our Agent Fabric platform separates concerns into three layers: the reasoning layer (an LLM/SLM that plans and decides), the execution layer (tools for browser control, API calls, DB queries, CI/CD integration), and the evaluation layer (QAVE, which assesses whether outputs meet quality and compliance standards). Each layer can be replaced or upgraded independently.

Where teams go wrong

The most common mistake is trying to automate 100% of test execution immediately. Agentic QE works best when introduced incrementally — starting with regression suites, then expanding to exploratory testing, then spec-to-test generation. Teams that "big bang" the transition end up with complex systems they can't debug.

Compliance • March 2025 • 10 min read

IRDAI's AI Guidelines: A Practical Compliance Guide

IRDAI's 2024 AI governance guidelines are not advisory — they carry examination implications. The guidelines cover five domains: fairness and non-discrimination, explainability, accountability, data governance, and operational resilience. Each domain has specific obligations that must be mapped to specific AI systems in production.

The fairness challenge

For underwriting and claims AI, fairness is the most immediate concern. The guidelines require that AI systems do not systematically disadvantage policyholders based on protected characteristics. The challenge is that fairness metrics are contested — a system can be statistically fair by one metric and unfair by another.

⚖️ Critical point: "We used a third-party model" is not an acceptable compliance defence. IRDAI holds insurers accountable for every AI system in their stack, regardless of who built it.

Explainability requirements

The guidelines require that AI decisions affecting policyholders be explainable in terms customers understand. Our recommendation is a two-level approach: technical explainability (SHAP values, policy reasoning traces for internal audit) and customer explainability (plain-language summary for customer communication).

What to do now

First, inventory every AI system touching a policyholder decision. Second, map each system against the IRDAI obligation library. Third, run a gap assessment and prioritize remediation by risk level. QAVE's CHEQ platform automates steps two and three, generating obligation-level scorecards with evidence ready for examiners.

SLMs • March 2025 • 7 min read

SLMs vs LLMs for Enterprise QE

The instinct to use the largest, most capable model available is understandable. This logic breaks down in enterprise QE contexts where cost, latency, compliance, and auditability are not afterthoughts.

What SLMs are good at

Small Language Models (1B–13B parameters) are faster, cheaper, and more controllable than frontier LLMs. For well-defined tasks with structured outputs — extracting test steps from a user story, classifying a defect, checking API response schemas — fine-tuned SLMs routinely outperform larger models. They've been trained on domain-specific data and tuned to produce exact output formats.

🏆 Benchmark: Our Insurance NER SLM achieves 94.2% F1 on policy documents vs 81.3% for GPT-4o zero-shot. It runs in 40ms vs 2.8s for the API call. Cost: 99% lower.

When to use LLMs

LLMs win when tasks require broad knowledge, complex multi-step reasoning, or handling highly varied inputs. Exploratory test generation from unstructured product descriptions and cross-domain analysis are cases where a large model's breadth matters more than a small model's efficiency.

The recommendation

Use a hybrid architecture: LLMs for planning and reasoning, SLMs for execution and judgment. The LLM designs the eval framework; the SLM runs it at scale.

Sovereign AI • February 2025 • 9 min read

Sovereign AI: Why Regulated Enterprises Can't Send Models Abroad

Every time your LLM application calls an external API, it sends data to servers outside your control, potentially outside your country's jurisdiction, and definitely outside your data governance framework. For regulated BFSI and healthcare in India, this is increasingly unacceptable.

The regulatory landscape

RBI guidelines on outsourcing, IRDAI's data localisation requirements, and India's DPDP Act collectively require regulated entities to know where their customer data is processed, stored, and who has access. AI inference is data processing under these frameworks.

🔒 Architecture principle: Sovereign AI doesn't mean building your own foundation model. It means deploying a foundation model within infrastructure that meets your data governance requirements, wrapped with the observability and compliance controls regulators expect.

The Qapitol approach

Our Sovereign AI framework combines open-weight model deployment (Llama, Mistral, or domain-fine-tuned SLMs), private Kubernetes infrastructure, QAVE evaluation on-premise, and CHEQ compliance monitoring — all within the client's VPC. Clients retain full control over models, data, and audit logs.

AI Engineering • February 2025 • 6 min read

RLHF in Production: The Gap Between Paper and Pipeline

RLHF is the technique that makes LLMs helpful rather than just capable. The research literature makes it sound tractable. The production reality involves annotator disagreement, reward model overfitting, distribution shift, and the uncomfortable reality that "human preference" is not a stable objective function.

The annotation problem

RLHF requires human annotators to compare model outputs. In practice, annotators disagree dramatically depending on their background and interpretation. For domain-specific applications like insurance, annotators need to be domain experts — making labelling expensive and slow.

📊 Finding: In our RLHF pipeline for insurance document QA, inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's kappa) was 0.67 with trained QE engineers but only 0.41 with general-purpose annotation services. The difference in downstream model quality was significant.

The reward hacking problem

The reward model is an approximation of human judgment — and the RL process is very good at finding outputs that score highly on the reward model without actually reflecting human preferences. This requires active monitoring and periodic reward model retraining.

Synthetic Data Management • January 2025 • 8 min read

Synthetic Data Management Is a Quality Problem

The standard pitch for synthetic data: "You can have as much test data as you want, without privacy risk." True. But it misses the harder question: how do you know the synthetic data is any good? Quantity is not quality. A test environment flooded with unrealistic synthetic data produces misleading test results.

What makes synthetic data good?

Good synthetic data has three properties: statistical fidelity (distributions match real data), referential integrity (relationships between entities are maintained), and edge case coverage (includes extreme cases that matter most). Achieving all three requires deliberate design — not just pressing "generate."

🔑 Key distinction: Random data is not synthetic data. Synthetic data is statistically modelled from real data — it should be as realistic as real data, without being real data. The validation pipeline is what separates the two.

The validation pipeline

Every synthetic dataset should pass through: statistical validation (KS tests, Wasserstein distance), business rule validation (domain constraints), and test effectiveness validation (would tests on synthetic data catch the same defects as tests on real data?).
